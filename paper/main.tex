\begin{abstract}
We study how much compute a small generative language model needs to become a competent
context-grounded question answerer, whether broader training data buys out-of-domain robustness,
and how to serve the result efficiently. Using Qwen3-0.6B, LoRA, and the MRQA benchmark collection
under a single controlled pipeline, we find that (i) adaptation is extremely cheap: 250k training
tokens (578 questions, 20 seconds on one GPU) raise mean F1 from 41.4 (3-shot prompting) to 70.6
in-domain and from 34.3 to 62.6 on unseen BioASQ and DuoRC, and every further $4\times$ increase in
budget adds only about 2 F1 up to 16M tokens; (ii) at a fixed 4M-token budget, mixing SQuAD, NewsQA
and TriviaQA does \emph{not} improve out-of-domain F1 over SQuAD-only training (paired differences
$-0.33$ and $+0.49$ F1, both 95\% CIs include zero over three seeds), contrary to our pre-registered
hypothesis, while it costs 1.2 F1 on SQuAD itself; and (iii) static batching raises inference throughput
$7.1\times$ (to 116 questions/s) at batch size 64 before it declines, at the cost of $9\times$ higher
latency, while length-sorting the batch nearly doubles throughput at large batch sizes and quality is
unchanged throughout. We release code, configurations, logs and per-question predictions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Small generative language models are attractive for question answering (QA) over a provided
passage: they run on a single commodity GPU, can be adapted cheaply, and produce free-form answers
without a task-specific span-extraction head. How much compute such adaptation needs, how well the
adapted model transfers to unseen domains, and how to serve it efficiently are practical questions
that are rarely answered together under one controlled setup.

We study them with \textbf{Qwen3-0.6B}~\citep{qwen3} adapted by low-rank adaptation
(LoRA)~\citep{hu2022lora} to context-grounded single-turn QA on the MRQA collection~\citep{fisch2019mrqa}.
All experiments share one model, one prompt, one LoRA configuration and one evaluation pipeline, and the
training data are controlled by an exact \emph{training-token budget}. We ask:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \RQ{1} (training efficiency): how does QA quality change with the LoRA training-token budget, relative to zero- and few-shot prompting?
  \item \RQ{2} (generalization): at a fixed budget, does multi-source fine-tuning (SQuAD + NewsQA + TriviaQA) generalize better to unseen domains (BioASQ, DuoRC) than SQuAD-only fine-tuning?
  \item \RQ{3} (inference efficiency): how does the inference batch size trade throughput against latency and GPU memory?
\end{itemize}
Hypotheses and quantitative predictions were fixed before the main runs; Appendix~\ref{app:hypotheses}
compares them with the outcomes.

\paragraph{Contributions.} (1) A controlled, fully reproducible pipeline for token-budgeted LoRA
fine-tuning and evaluation of a small generative QA model, including passage-level splits, overlap
filtering, paired-bootstrap statistics and error analysis. (2) Evidence that the step from prompting to
\emph{any} fine-tuning dominates the returns: 0.25M tokens recover most of the attainable F1 and further
budget yields a roughly log-linear $\approx$2 F1 per $4\times$ tokens. (3) A negative result on data
diversity: at equal token budgets, multi-source training does not improve out-of-domain F1 for this
model, and per-source validation loss is a misleading proxy for cross-domain answer quality.
(4) An inference characterisation showing an interior throughput optimum for static batching and the
large effect of length-sorting.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Parameter-efficient adaptation.} LoRA~\citep{hu2022lora} trains low-rank updates to frozen
weight matrices and is the default way to adapt LLMs on limited hardware; QLoRA~\citep{dettmers2023qlora}
additionally quantizes the frozen base. A small amount of supervised data can suffice to change a
model's output behaviour~\citep{zhou2023lima}; we quantify this for context-grounded QA in units of
training tokens and GPU time.

\paragraph{Generalization in reading comprehension.} The MRQA shared task~\citep{fisch2019mrqa}
standardised six training and six held-out QA datasets to measure out-of-domain transfer. Training on
several sources improved the robustness of extractive models~\citep{talmor2019multiqa,fisch2019mrqa},
and multi-format training benefits generative QA~\citep{khashabi2020unifiedqa,raffel2020t5}. Such
comparisons usually confound diversity with data quantity; we hold the token budget fixed. Domain
shift and continued adaptation are discussed by \citet{gururangan2020dontstop}.

\paragraph{Compute budgets and inference.} Scaling studies relate loss to parameters and training
tokens~\citep{kaplan2020scaling,hoffmann2022chinchilla}. Autoregressive decoding is memory-bound, so
batching amortizes weight reads~\citep{pope2023inference}; serving systems such as Orca~\citep{yu2022orca}
and vLLM~\citep{kwon2023vllm} schedule requests dynamically and IO-aware kernels speed up
attention~\citep{dao2022flashattention}. We measure the basic static-batching trade-off these systems
build on.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Data.} We use the MRQA 2019 versions~\citep{fisch2019mrqa} of SQuAD~\citep{rajpurkar2016squad},
NewsQA~\citep{trischler2017newsqa} and TriviaQA~\citep{joshi2017triviaqa} (web) for training and
in-domain (ID) evaluation, and BioASQ~\citep{tsatsaronis2015bioasq} and DuoRC
(ParaphraseRC)~\citep{saha2018duorc} as out-of-domain (OOD) test sets that are never used for training
or model selection. Training pools are capped at 50k questions per source. From each pool we hold out
whole passages (300 questions) as a development set used only for validation loss. ID test sets come
from the MRQA validation splits and OOD sets from the MRQA test split. Eval questions whose passage
also occurs in a training pool (after whitespace and case normalisation) are removed before sampling
1,000 questions per set; this removed 552 NewsQA and 601 TriviaQA candidates
(Table~\ref{tab:data} in Appendix~\ref{app:data}). MRQA markup tokens are converted to plain text.

\paragraph{Task format and model.} Each input is a single chat turn: a system instruction asking for
the shortest answer span copied from the passage, followed by \texttt{Passage: \dots\ Question: \dots}.
Qwen3's thinking mode is disabled and passages are truncated to 768 tokens (at test time the head of
the passage is kept; for training the window is moved to contain the answer). Few-shot prompting
prepends three fixed short SQuAD training examples as earlier chat turns~\citep{brown2020gpt3}. Answers
are decoded greedily (at most 32 tokens) and the first line is kept.

\paragraph{LoRA fine-tuning under a token budget.} Adapters of rank 16 ($\alpha{=}32$, dropout 0.05)
are attached to all attention and MLP projections: 10.1M trainable parameters, 1.7\% of the 606M total.
We use AdamW, learning rate $2{\times}10^{-4}$, 5\% warm-up and cosine decay, 16 sequences per
optimizer step and the loss on answer tokens only. A budget $B$ means that examples are drawn without
replacement---with an equal token share per source---until their prompt plus answer tokens sum to $B$;
the model makes a single pass with a schedule fitted to that length. Cost is reported as wall-clock GPU
time and estimated FLOPs $\approx 4NT$ for $N$ parameters and $T$ tokens (forward plus activation
gradients; the frozen base needs no weight gradients). \RQ{1} trains on the three-source mixture with
$B \in \{0.25, 1, 4, 16\}$M tokens. \RQ{2} compares SQuAD-only training with the three-source mixture
at $B{=}4$M over three seeds (data sample and adapter initialisation); the mixture's seed-0 run is shared
with \RQ{1}.

\paragraph{Evaluation and statistics.} We report Exact Match (EM) and token-level F1 with the official
SQuAD/MRQA normalisation, maximised over gold answers. Confidence intervals are 95\% percentile
bootstrap intervals over questions~\citep{efron1993bootstrap}. System comparisons use a paired bootstrap
over the same 1,000 questions~\citep{koehn2004bootstrap} with 10,000 resamples, applied to per-question
scores averaged over seeds; we also report the seed-to-seed standard deviation~\citep{dodge2020finetuning}.

\paragraph{Inference benchmark.} \RQ{3} decodes 512 SQuAD prompts with the merged 16M-token adapter in
bf16 using HuggingFace \texttt{generate}~\citep{wolf2020transformers}: batch sizes 1--256 with
length-sorted, left-padded batches and the KV cache on, plus ablations with unsorted batches, the KV cache
disabled (64-prompt subset) and fp16/fp32 weights. Each configuration has one warm-up batch and three
timed repeats (run-to-run standard deviation below 0.3\% of throughput). Latency is the wall-clock time
of the batch that contains a request.

\paragraph{Hardware.} All final runs used one NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell Server Edition GPU (96~GB;
Google Colab ``G4'') with PyTorch, Transformers~\citep{wolf2020transformers} and PEFT~\citep{peft}.
All LoRA training for this paper took 48 GPU-minutes.

Table~\ref{tab:rq1} and Figure~\ref{fig:rq1} show that the largest gain comes from the first, smallest
budget. Zero-shot, the model answers in full sentences (EM 2--8\%); three demonstrations raise mean F1
to 41.4 ID and 34.3 OOD. Fine-tuning on only 250k tokens---578 questions and 20 seconds of training---lifts
mean F1 to 70.6 ID ($+29.2$) and 62.6 OOD ($+28.3$), with gains on every set (SQuAD $+26.8$, BioASQ $+28.8$,
DuoRC $+27.8$). Beyond that, returns are modest and roughly log-linear: averaged over the five sets, F1 rises
from 67.4 (250k) to 68.4 (1M), 71.4 (4M) and 73.8 (16M), i.e.\ about 2 F1 per $4\times$ more tokens,
so the 64$\times$ larger 16M budget adds 7.7 ID and 4.4 OOD points for 60$\times$ the compute. The OOD
curve flattens earlier than the ID curve: BioASQ peaks at 4M (74.3 vs.\ 74.2 at 16M), whereas NewsQA,
which has the longest answers, keeps improving ($56.5 \rightarrow 67.9$).

Validation loss decreases monotonically with the budget (0.84, 0.66, 0.56, 0.48 nats per answer token;

took 20 minutes and $3.9{\times}10^{16}$ FLOPs (Figure~\ref{fig:rq1cost}).

\label{sec:rq2}

  Mean $\pm$ std over 3 seeds; $\Delta$F1 is multi minus single with a 95\% paired-bootstrap CI over
  questions (scores averaged over seeds) and a two-sided $p$-value.}
  \label{tab:rq2}
  \centering
  \small
  \resizebox{\linewidth}{!}{\input{tables/rq2.tex}}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:rq2} answers \RQ{2} negatively for this model and budget. On the two unseen domains the
multi-source mixture is statistically indistinguishable from SQuAD-only training: $\Delta$F1 $=-0.33$
\ci{-1.40}{+0.77} on BioASQ and $+0.49$ \ci{-0.80}{+1.79} on DuoRC, and the mean OOD F1 is 66.2 for both
conditions. The seed-to-seed standard deviation (0.6--1.2 F1) is as large as the differences, and
individual seeds point both ways (Appendix~\ref{app:extra}): on DuoRC, one seed favours the mixture by
$+2.4$ F1 ($p{=}0.01$) and the other two favour SQuAD by about 0.5. Only a single-seed comparison would
have suggested a significant effect. In domain, the mixture helps a little on the two sources it adds
(NewsQA $+1.2$, $p{=}0.07$; TriviaQA $+1.0$, $p{=}0.32$) and costs 1.2 F1 on SQuAD
\ci{-2.07}{-0.29} ($p{=}0.008$), the only significant difference.

\paragraph{Why no OOD gain?} Two observations explain part of it. First, equal tokens are not equal
supervision: SQuAD passages are short, so the SQuAD-only run sees 16.8k questions and 102k answer tokens,
versus 9.2k questions and 54k answer tokens for the mixture, and it trains 38\% faster (18.4k vs.\ 13.3k
tokens/s). Second, the base model already reads well; what fine-tuning mostly teaches is the
\emph{answer format}---short spans copied from the passage---and SQuAD alone teaches it. The error analysis
(Table~\ref{tab:errors}) supports this: the mixture produces shorter answers (mean predicted length on
TriviaQA 1.97 $\rightarrow$ 1.55 words, BioASQ 1.88 $\rightarrow$ 1.73) and fewer \emph{verbose} answers
(TriviaQA 9.9\% $\rightarrow$ 3.9\%, BioASQ 10.6\% $\rightarrow$ 7.5\%), but these mostly turn into
\emph{partial} matches rather than exact ones, and the dominant OOD failure---choosing the wrong span
(13.6--13.8\% on BioASQ, 27.8--28.2\% on DuoRC)---is unchanged. A further 7\% of DuoRC answers fall outside
the 768-token window and are unanswerable as posed.

\paragraph{Validation loss is a poor cross-domain proxy.} The SQuAD-only adapters have a mean dev loss
of 1.25 versus 0.55 for the mixture, driven by a TriviaQA dev loss of 2.68 vs.\ 0.89. Yet their TriviaQA
F1 is only 1.0 point lower. TriviaQA gold answers are lower-cased aliases; the SQuAD-only model assigns
low probability to that surface form but still produces the right entity, which F1 normalisation forgives.
Model selection by pooled dev loss would therefore strongly, and wrongly, favour the mixture.

Throughput grows from 16.4 questions/s at batch size 1 to a maximum of 116.1 at batch size 64 ($7.1\times$)
and then \emph{declines} to 102 (128) and 81 (256) (Figure~\ref{fig:rq3}, Table~\ref{tab:rq3}). The decline
has two causes visible in the logs: with static batching every sequence waits for the longest answer in
its batch, and at large batch sizes the length spread inside a batch grows, so padding rises to 30--48\%
of prompt tokens even after sorting. Latency grows faster than throughput: a request waits 543~ms at batch
64 (9$\times$ batch 1) and 3.1~s at batch 256. Peak memory grows almost linearly with batch size, from
1.2~GB (weights 1.1~GB) to 7.0~GB at 64 and 24.6~GB at 256, dominated by the KV cache and prefill
activations of 700-token prompts. Quality is unaffected: F1 stays within 90.3--90.9 across all batch sizes.

Length sorting is the cheapest optimisation: unsorted batches carry 46--65\% padding at batch $\geq 16$ and
lose up to 48\% of throughput (60.9 vs.\ 116.1 questions/s at batch 64), with their optimum shifting down to
batch 32. Disabling the KV cache costs nothing at batch 1---answers are only about five tokens long, so
decoding is dominated by the prefill---but is $1.7\times$ slower at batch 8 and $5.1\times$ slower at batch 32
(Table~\ref{tab:rq3abl}). fp16 matches bf16 in speed and quality, while fp32 doubles memory and is
$1.4\times$ slower at batch 32.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

For a 0.6B model on context-grounded QA, the practical lesson is that adaptation compute is not the
bottleneck: a few hundred examples close most of the gap to a much longer run, and a 16M-token run costs
20 GPU-minutes. Because the gain beyond the smallest budget is log-linear and smaller out of domain, a
practitioner with a fixed budget would do better spending it on evaluation, seeds and data quality than
on longer training. Our \RQ{2} result cautions against assuming that data diversity transfers: at matched
tokens, diversity traded answer-format calibration for in-domain accuracy without moving OOD F1, and with a
single seed we would have drawn a different conclusion.

\paragraph{Limitations.} We study one base model, one LoRA configuration and one prompt; larger models or
other adapters may behave differently. Budgets up to 16M tokens are small compared with the full MRQA
training sets, and diversity may matter more at larger budgets or with more distant sources. The equal-token
design gives SQuAD-only training more questions and answer tokens, so ``diversity at equal supervision'' is
not what \RQ{2} measures. Each set has 1,000 questions, so differences of about one F1 point are at the
resolution of our CIs; EM/F1 penalise correct paraphrases, and overlap filtering uses exact normalised
matches only. \RQ{3} uses HuggingFace \texttt{generate} with static batching on a single GPU type; serving
engines with continuous batching and paged attention~\citep{kwon2023vllm} would shift the throughput optimum.
Finally, our hypotheses were pre-registered against a T4 configuration and the final runs used a larger GPU
profile (Appendix~\ref{app:hypotheses}).

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A 0.6B-parameter model becomes a strong extractive-style QA system with remarkably little fine-tuning:
250k LoRA training tokens add about 29 F1 over few-shot prompting both in and out of domain, and further
budget yields about 2 F1 per $4\times$ tokens. Mixing source datasets at a fixed token budget did not improve
transfer to biomedical or movie-plot questions, and pooled validation loss would have suggested otherwise.
At inference, length-sorted static batching at batch size 32--64 gives a $6.5$--$7\times$ throughput gain
with sub-second latency and unchanged quality.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.} Every number in this paper is produced by
\texttt{python -m cegqa.cli all --config configs/default.yaml --config configs/a100.yaml} from the released
repository, which also writes the tables and figures used here; \texttt{results/} contains the metrics,
training logs, statistics and benchmark rows behind them. The notebooks in \texttt{notebooks/} run the same
pipeline on Google Colab.

\paragraph{Author contributions.} P1 built the data and evaluation pipeline (MRQA processing, passage-level
splits, overlap checks, EM/F1 and prediction logging). P2 built the prompting baselines and the LoRA training
pipeline with token-budget sampling and cost accounting (\RQ{1}). P3 designed and analysed the generalization
study, including the paired bootstrap and error analysis (\RQ{2}). P4 built the inference benchmark (\RQ{3}).
P5 led the analysis, figures and writing. All authors interpreted the results and edited the paper.

\section{Pre-registered hypotheses and outcomes}
\label{app:hypotheses}

Hypotheses were fixed in \texttt{docs/hypotheses.md} before the main runs, for a T4 configuration
(budgets up to 4M, RQ2 at 1M, 500 questions per set). The final runs used the large-GPU profile
(budgets up to 16M, RQ2 at 4M, 1,000 questions per set); the predictions are evaluated unchanged.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{H1a} (250k tokens beat the best prompting baseline on every ID set; $\geq$15 F1 on SQuAD):
  \emph{supported} ($+26.8$ F1 on SQuAD; $+25.0$ NewsQA; $+35.8$ TriviaQA).
  \item \textbf{H1b} (diminishing returns; 1M$\rightarrow$4M adds less than half of 0.25M$\rightarrow$1M):
  \emph{not supported}. Gains per $4\times$ budget are roughly constant (+1.0, +3.0, +2.4 mean F1), i.e.\ F1 is
  close to linear in log tokens, which is the functional form we predicted, but without the predicted decay.
  \item \textbf{H1c} (dev loss decreases monotonically and ranks runs like dev F1): \emph{supported} for mean ID F1;
  TriviaQA alone is non-monotonic.
  \item \textbf{H1d} (tokens/s within $\pm$10\% across budgets): \emph{supported} (12.7--13.3k tokens/s).
  \item \textbf{H2a} (multi-source improves mean OOD F1 by 2--5 points with a CI excluding 0 on at least one OOD set):
  \emph{not supported} ($+0.08$ mean OOD F1; both CIs include 0).
  \item \textbf{H2b} (gain larger on DuoRC than BioASQ): directionally consistent ($+0.49$ vs.\ $-0.33$) but not significant.
  \item \textbf{H2c} (SQuAD-only within $\pm$2 F1 on SQuAD): \emph{supported} (SQuAD-only better by 1.2).
  \item \textbf{H2d} (multi-source mainly reduces not-in-passage and wrong-span errors): \emph{not supported}; it
  mainly reduces verbose answers, and wrong-span errors are unchanged.
  \item \textbf{H3a} ($\geq 8\times$ throughput, saturating at batch 32--64): \emph{partly supported} ($7.1\times$, peak at 64).
  \item \textbf{H3b} (latency at batch 64 $\geq 3\times$ batch 1): \emph{supported} ($9.0\times$).
  \item \textbf{H3c} (memory roughly linear in batch size, below 16~GB up to 64): \emph{supported} (7.0~GB at 64).
  \item \textbf{H3d} (quality within $\pm$0.5 F1; unsorted $\geq$15\% slower at large batches; KV cache off $\geq 2\times$
  slower): \emph{supported} for quality ($\pm$0.3) and sorting (48\% slower at 64); \emph{partly} for the KV cache
  ($5.1\times$ at batch 32, but no difference at batch 1 because answers are short).
\end{itemize}
